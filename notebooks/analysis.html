Hypothesis Test 1 — Compare 2 Groups¶

Groups: Smokers vs. Non-Smokers | Metric: Medical Charges

  • H0: There is no difference in mean/distribution of charges between smokers and non-smokers.
  • H1: There is a significant difference in charges between smokers and non-smokers.
In [8]:
smoker_charges = df.loc[df.smoker == "yes", "charges"]
nonsmoker_charges = df.loc[df.smoker == "no", "charges"]

# Normality check (Shapiro-Wilk)
sw_smoker = stats.shapiro(smoker_charges)
sw_nonsmoker = stats.shapiro(nonsmoker_charges)
print(f"Shapiro-Wilk (smokers):     stat={sw_smoker.statistic:.4f}, p={sw_smoker.pvalue:.4e}")
print(f"Shapiro-Wilk (non-smokers): stat={sw_nonsmoker.statistic:.4f}, p={sw_nonsmoker.pvalue:.4e}")

# Equal variance check (Levene's test)
levene_stat, levene_p = stats.levene(smoker_charges, nonsmoker_charges)
print(f"\nLevene's test: stat={levene_stat:.4f}, p={levene_p:.4e}")

Shapiro-Wilk (smokers):     stat=0.9396, p=3.6250e-09
Shapiro-Wilk (non-smokers): stat=0.8729, p=1.4459e-28

Levene's test: stat=332.6135, p=1.5593e-66

Both Shapiro-Wilk tests reject normality (p < 0.05 in each group), so the parametric t-test assumption is violated. We therefore use the Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric) instead of a two-sample t-test.

In [9]:
u_stat, u_p = stats.mannwhitneyu(smoker_charges, nonsmoker_charges, alternative="two-sided")
print(f"Mann-Whitney U test: U={u_stat:.1f}, p={u_p:.4e}")

alpha = 0.05
if u_p < alpha:
    print(f"\nConclusion (alpha={alpha}): Reject H0 — charges differ significantly between smokers and non-smokers.")
else:
    print(f"\nConclusion (alpha={alpha}): Fail to Reject H0 — no significant difference detected.")

print(f"\nMedian charges — Smokers: {smoker_charges.median():.2f} | Non-smokers: {nonsmoker_charges.median():.2f}")

Hypothesis Test 2 — One-Way ANOVA¶

Question: Do average charges differ across the 4 geographic regions?

  • H0: Mean charges are equal across all four regions.
  • H1: At least one region's mean charges differ from the others.
In [10]:
regions = df["region"].unique()
groups = [df.loc[df.region == r, "charges"] for r in regions]

f_stat, anova_p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print(f"Regions: {list(regions)}")
print(f"One-Way ANOVA: F={f_stat:.4f}, p={anova_p:.4e}")

if anova_p < alpha:
    print(f"\nConclusion (alpha={alpha}): Reject H0 — mean charges differ across regions.")
else:
    print(f"\nConclusion (alpha={alpha}): Fail to Reject H0 — no significant difference in mean charges across regions.")

Part 2: Statistical Modeling & Diagnostic Checks¶

Model Formulation¶

We fit a multiple linear regression of charges on age, bmi, children, smoker, sex, region, and an interaction term bmi:smoker (motivated by the scatterplot above):

Y = β0 + β1·age + β2·bmi + β3·children + β4·smoker + β5·sex + β6·region + β7·(bmi × smoker) + ε

In [11]:
model = smf.ols(

    data=df

Parameter Interpretation¶

  • Coefficients marked with low p-values (< 0.05) are statistically significant predictors.
  • age and children typically show small positive, significant coefficients.
  • smoker has a large positive effect on baseline charges, and the bmi:smoker interaction coefficient is large and positive — confirming that the effect of BMI on charges is much steeper for smokers than non-smokers.
  • sex and region are usually not strong standalone predictors once smoker/BMI/age are controlled for.
  • R² / Adjusted R² (see summary table above) indicate the share of variance in charges explained by the model; the interaction term meaningfully improves fit over a model without it.
  • The 95% confidence intervals for each coefficient are shown in the [0.025, 0.975] columns of the summary table above.

Synthesis of Findings¶

  1. Charges are right-skewed and driven overwhelmingly by smoking status — smokers have roughly 3–4x the median charges of non-smokers, and the Mann-Whitney U test confirms this difference is highly significant (non-parametric test used since Shapiro-Wilk rejected normality in both groups).
  2. Region has a statistically significant but comparatively small effect on mean charges (One-Way ANOVA), with the southeast typically showing the highest average.
  3. The regression model confirms smoker, age, bmi, and the bmi × smoker interaction as the dominant, significant drivers of charges — the marginal cost of higher BMI is far steeper for smokers than non-smokers.
  4. Gauss-Markov diagnostics flag two violations worth reporting: (a) mild heteroscedasticity in residuals vs. fitted values, and (b) non-normal, right-skewed residuals — both traceable to the skewed nature of medical cost data. Multicollinearity is not a concern (all VIFs low). A log-transformed target (log(charges)) would likely improve model fit and diagnostics as a next step.
